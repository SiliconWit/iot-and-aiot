Before a wind turbine is installed, someone has to measure the wind at the site for at least a year. That has created a steady demand for cup anemometers and wind vanes. Many commercial instruments are on sale, but some lack proper calibration, and many are too expensive for small-scale wind farmers, especially in developing countries.

Wind data is also a large-data problem. A year of readings from several masts is a long, continuous record, and most commercial instruments are not built to handle it that way.

## What we did

We designed a cup-vane instrument for collecting wind data and made its parts by 3D printing. The instrument works as a wireless sensor node in an Internet of Things (IoT) network. It acquires wind data, stores it, and sends it wirelessly to a sink node using the IEEE 802.15.4 protocol.

We then calibrated the instrument. In the laboratory we tested it in an Armfield wind tunnel at wind speeds from 1 to 16 m/s. Outside, we tested it in an open field at wind speeds from 1 to 5 m/s.

## What we found

- The instrument acquired wind data within a mean fitting deviation of plus or minus 0.063398 m/s.
- It stored the data and presented it wirelessly to an IEEE 802.15.4 sink node.
- Its performance was verified in the wind tunnel across 1 to 16 m/s and in the open field across 1 to 5 m/s.

## Why it matters

For a small-scale wind farmer, the first cost of a wind project is the year of measurement. An instrument that can be 3D-printed, calibrated against a wind tunnel and read over a wireless link is meant to lower that cost. Because each instrument is a node in a wireless network, several masts can report to one sink, which suits the large, continuous data sets that wind assessment needs.

This instrument later became the field node of a complete wind resource assessment system, which also analyses the data and reports wind distributions and power predictions.

## Limits

This summary is written from the published abstract. The outdoor test covered only low wind speeds, 1 to 5 m/s; higher speeds were tested in the wind tunnel. The abstract does not report a long field record, the durability of the printed parts outdoors, or a cost comparison with commercial instruments.
