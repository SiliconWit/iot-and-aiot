In many developing regions, smallholder farmers face growing water scarcity. Advice on saving water often starts with technology: sensors, controllers and wireless links. For a farmer with little cash and no technician nearby, that is the hardest place to start. Simpler methods, such as planting crops together or running drip lines fed by gravity, are easier to adopt.

The farmer needs to know two things: how much water each approach saves, alone and together, and which one to adopt first.

## What we did

We combined three approaches: IoT precision irrigation, intercropping, and gravity-fed drip irrigation.

In the laboratory, we ran experiments with an ATmega2560 microcontroller and environmental sensors across three upland crop systems. We also designed a scalable architecture for field deployment, using STM32 microcontrollers with CC1101 radio modules to link the field nodes.

We then used mathematical modelling to estimate the water savings of each approach and of their combinations. Finally, we assessed how feasible each approach is to put into practice on a smallholder farm, and ranked them.

## What we found

- IoT precision irrigation alone saves 8.6% of water.
- Intercropping combined with IoT saves 19.0%.
- Gravity-fed drip irrigation saves 22% as a baseline.
- IoT combined with gravity-fed drip saves 28.7%.
- Complete integration of all three saves 36.9%.
- On feasibility, intercropping ranked highest because its barriers to adoption are low. IoT systems ranked lowest, despite their technical benefits.

## Why it matters

The largest saving comes from combining the methods, not from any one of them. The method that is easiest to adopt is part of that combination. This gives a farmer, an extension officer or a project funder an order of work.

We propose a phased roadmap. Start with the accessible techniques, such as intercropping, which have low barriers to adoption. Add IoT precision irrigation later, once the simpler steps are in place, to reach the full saving. This lets smallholders gain part of the benefit early and invest in technology when it adds to what they already have.

## Limits

This summary is written from the published abstract. The water savings come from mathematical modelling built on laboratory experiments, not from measured field seasons. The field architecture with STM32 and CC1101 radios was designed, but the abstract does not report a field deployment. The feasibility ranking is an assessment, not a survey of farmers' choices. Further research is needed on adaptations for specific climates and on economic optimization models before wider use.
